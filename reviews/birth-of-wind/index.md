## The review

Reviewer: Dan Holloway  
Outlet: Pacific Book Review

Stephen Francis Montagna’s The Birth of Wind, the opening installment of the Wind Trilogy, is an ambitious and sweeping work that blends mythological origins, historical depth, and epic storytelling into a richly layered narrative. Drawing heavily from Japanese creation mythology and early feudal history, Montagna crafts a novel that is both expansive in scope and deeply rooted in cultural lore.

From its opening prologue, the novel immerses readers in the ancient mythos of Izanagi and Izanami, the divine figures responsible for the creation of Japan and its pantheon of gods. This mythological foundation is not merely decorative—it serves as the philosophical and thematic backbone of the story, establishing a sense of destiny, chaos, and divine influence that resonates throughout the narrative. The author’s detailed rendering of these origins demonstrates both research and reverence, offering readers a vivid entry point into a world shaped by gods, war, and elemental forces.

As the story transitions from myth to recorded history, Montagna maintains a strong narrative presence, guiding readers through the turbulent evolution of feudal Japan. The novel explores the rise of powerful clans, the emergence of the Samurai class, and the brutal conflicts that defined the era. Historical events—such as the Gempei War and the establishment of the shogunate—are depicted with intensity and clarity, giving the novel a grounded authenticity that complements its mythic beginnings.

One of the book’s greatest strengths lies in its ambition. Montagna does not shy away from complexity; instead, he embraces it, presenting a dense and detailed account of political intrigue, warfare, and cultural transformation. His prose carries a commanding tone, often echoing the cadence of epic chronicles, which reinforces the grandeur of the story. Readers who appreciate historically driven narratives with a mythological edge will find much to admire here.

However, the novel’s depth can also present challenges. The extensive historical exposition and large cast of figures may require careful attention, particularly for readers less familiar with Japanese history. At times, the pacing leans heavily toward informational detail rather than character-driven momentum, which may momentarily distance readers seeking a more intimate narrative focus.

Despite this, The Birth of Wind stands as a bold and immersive introduction to the trilogy. It is a work that prioritizes scale, legacy, and the forces—both divine and human—that shape civilizations. Montagna’s vision is clear: to tell not just a story, but the story behind a world forged in myth, conflict, and enduring tradition.

### Pacific Book Review Verdict:

The Birth of Wind is a compelling and intellectually rich novel that successfully bridges mythology and history. With its epic tone and meticulous detail, it offers a rewarding experience for readers drawn to grand narratives and cultural depth.
